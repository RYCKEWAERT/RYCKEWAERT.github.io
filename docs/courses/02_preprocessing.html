Chemometrics Part 2 — Spectral Preprocessing

Savitzky-Golay smoothing and Standard Normal Variate transformation

Learning objectives

After completing this module you should be able to:

  1. Explain why raw NIR spectra require preprocessing before regression modelling.
  2. Apply Savitzky-Golay smoothing and articulate its advantage over moving-average.
  3. Derive the SNV formula and implement it as a row-wise normalisation.
  4. Compare the effect of each method visually and select appropriate preprocessing by cross-validation.

Dataset citation: Ryckewaert, M. et al. (2023). Dataset containing spectral data from hyperspectral imaging and sugar content measurements of grape berries in various maturity stages. Data in Brief. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dib.2022.108822

Introduction

Raw reflectance spectra are rarely fed directly into regression models. Two sources of unwanted variation affect NIR spectra of intact biological samples:

  1. High-frequency noise — detector noise, shot noise, small mechanical vibrations.
  2. Multiplicative and additive scatter — differences in particle size, path length, and surface geometry cause the entire spectrum to shift or scale, independently of chemical composition.

Two standard methods address these issues:

  • Savitzky-Golay (SG) smoothing — polynomial smoothing filter for noise reduction.
  • Standard Normal Variate (SNV) — row-wise standardisation for scatter correction.
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
from scipy.signal import savgol_filter
import plotly.graph_objects as go
from plotly.subplots import make_subplots

NAVY  = '#1b3a5c'
TEAL  = '#2a7f7f'
GREEN = '#78BE20'
GRAY  = '#868e96'
df = pd.read_csv('data/chemometrics/DATASET.csv', sep=';', index_col=0)

spectral_cols = [c for c in df.columns if c.startswith('x.')]
X_raw = df[spectral_cols].values
wavelengths = np.array([float(c.replace('x.', '', 1)) for c in spectral_cols])

# 12 evenly-spaced representative samples for consistent visualisation
n_show = 12
idx = np.linspace(0, len(X_raw) - 1, n_show, dtype=int)

print(f"Spectral matrix: {X_raw.shape}  (samples × bands)")
Spectral matrix: (274, 204)  (samples × bands)

Savitzky-Golay smoothing

The Savitzky-Golay filter fits a polynomial of degree \(d\) to a sliding window of \(w\) consecutive spectral points. The filtered value at position \(k\) is the polynomial evaluated at the centre of the window.

Why prefer SG over a simple moving average? A polynomial fit preserves peak positions and relative heights — a simple moving average distorts them by weighting all points equally.

Parameters used here: window_length = 11 (~30 nm), polyorder = 2, deriv = 0 (smoothing).

X_sg = savgol_filter(X_raw, window_length=11, polyorder=2, deriv=0, axis=1)

fig = make_subplots(rows=1, cols=2,
                    subplot_titles=['Raw spectra', 'After Savitzky-Golay (w=11, p=2)'])
for data, col in [(X_raw, 1), (X_sg, 2)]:
    for i in idx:
        fig.add_trace(
            go.Scatter(x=wavelengths, y=data[i], mode='lines',
                       line=dict(width=0.9), opacity=0.7,
                       showlegend=False, hoverinfo='skip'),
            row=1, col=col
        )
fig.update_xaxes(title_text='Wavelength (nm)')
fig.update_yaxes(title_text='Reflectance', col=1)
fig.update_layout(title='Effect of Savitzky-Golay smoothing',
                  template='simple_white', height=380)
fig.show()

Standard Normal Variate (SNV)

SNV standardises each spectrum independently (row-wise). For a spectrum \(\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \ldots, x_p)^\top\):

\[x_{\text{SNV},\,k} = \frac{x_k - \bar{x}}{\sigma_x}\]

where \(\bar{x} = \dfrac{1}{p}\sum_{k=1}^{p} x_k\) is the spectral mean and \(\sigma_x = \sqrt{\dfrac{1}{p-1}\sum_{k=1}^{p}(x_k - \bar{x})^2}\) the spectral standard deviation.

After SNV, every spectrum has zero mean and unit standard deviation along the wavelength axis, removing multiplicative and additive scatter effects without smoothing spectral features.

def snv(X):
    mean = X.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    std  = X.std(axis=1, ddof=1, keepdims=True)
    return (X - mean) / std

X_snv = snv(X_raw)

print(f"Mean of per-spectrum means: {X_snv.mean(axis=1).mean():.2e}  (expected ≈ 0)")
print(f"Mean of per-spectrum stds:  {X_snv.std(axis=1, ddof=1).mean():.6f}  (expected = 1)")
Mean of per-spectrum means: -1.80e-17  (expected ≈ 0)
Mean of per-spectrum stds:  1.000000  (expected = 1)
fig = go.Figure()
for i in idx:
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=wavelengths, y=X_snv[i], mode='lines',
                             line=dict(width=0.9), opacity=0.65,
                             showlegend=False, hoverinfo='skip'))
fig.add_hline(y=0, line_dash='dash', line_color=GRAY, line_width=0.8)
fig.update_layout(xaxis_title='Wavelength (nm)',
                  yaxis_title='SNV-standardised reflectance',
                  title='SNV-transformed spectra (12 samples)',

fig.show()

Three-panel comparison: Raw — Savitzky-Golay — SNV

The three panels show the same 12 samples under each preprocessing. Note:

  • SG reduces noise while preserving the absolute reflectance level
  • SNV flattens the overall reflectance range, making spectral shapes comparable but removing absolute intensity information
fig = make_subplots(rows=1, cols=3,

datasets = [X_raw, X_sg, X_snv]

Summary

Method Corrects Preserves Typical use case
Savitzky-Golay High-frequency noise Peak shape and position Always useful; required before derivatives
SNV Multiplicative and additive scatter Spectral shape (relative) Intact tissue, powders, solid samples

In practice, SG and SNV are often applied sequentially (SG first, then SNV). The optimal combination should be selected by cross-validation on the calibration set.
