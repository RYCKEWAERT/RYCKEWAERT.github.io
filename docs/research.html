Research

My research develops machine learning and statistical methods for biological systems that produce high-dimensional, heterogeneous data — from DNA sequences and spectral measurements to satellite imagery and occurrence records. A unifying thread across application domains is the multi-scale structure of biological variation: from the genome, through the individual organ (leaf, root, fruit), to the canopy and the landscape. Exploiting that structure requires methods that can fuse information across scales and modalities while remaining interpretable to biologists and breeders.

Motivation

Two converging pressures define the context of this work.

The data abundance problem. High-throughput phenotyping platforms, genomic arrays, and remote sensing now generate data at scales that exceed classical statistical workflows. A single UAV flight over a trial produces gigabytes of hyperspectral imagery; a genotyping array characterises hundreds of thousands of loci per individual. The bottleneck has shifted from data collection to extraction of reliable biological signal from these streams.

The environmental urgency. Perennial crops — grapevine, tropical trees, and others with multi-decade production cycles — face mounting pressure from climate change. Breeding cycles for these species span 10–20 years, making early, accurate prediction of genotype performance under future environments a critical bottleneck for food security and agroecosystem sustainability. Similarly, biodiversity monitoring requires scalable, automated tools to track species distribution shifts across large spatial extents and over time.

Standard machine learning approaches — developed for clean, balanced, large-scale benchmarks — transfer poorly to these contexts, which are characterised by small annotated datasets, heterogeneous measurement conditions, missing modalities, and strong domain-shift between training and deployment environments.

Open Questions

Multi-modal fusion across biological scales

Biological phenotypes emerge from the interplay of genetic, physiological, and environmental processes operating at different spatial and temporal scales. No single sensor captures all relevant variation. The central methodological question is: how can information from complementary modalities — genomic markers, spectral reflectance, morphological measurements, climatic time series — be fused in a principled way when modalities are partially observed and measured on different subsets of individuals?

Existing approaches (early fusion, late fusion, kernel methods) make strong assumptions about data alignment that rarely hold in field experiments. Architectures that handle missing modalities and learn cross-modal correspondences in a self-supervised regime are an open research direction.

Interpretability under biological constraints

Black-box predictions are not sufficient for plant breeding or ecological management: decisions must be justified to stakeholders and grounded in biological knowledge. What architectural and training choices make deep models interpretable without sacrificing predictive performance? Specific questions include the identifiability of latent representations, the faithfulness of attribution methods (SHAP, integrated gradients) on tabular and spectral data, and the design of models that encode domain-specific invariances (e.g. spectral wavelength ordering, phylogenetic structure).

New AI methods for ecological inference

Species distribution models must extrapolate beyond observed conditions — to novel climates, unstudied regions, or future time horizons. How can neural network-based SDMs produce calibrated uncertainty estimates that reflect genuine extrapolation risk, rather than interpolation confidence? Work building on the maximum entropy principle (MaxEnt) applied to neural architectures offers a tractable route: by constraining the learned distribution to maximum entropy subject to feature constraints, the model defaults to conservatism in data-sparse regions rather than overconfident extrapolation.

Approach

From genome to leaf to canopy

The multi-scale structure of plant phenotypes motivates a hierarchical research programme:

Scale Data types Key questions
Genome SNP arrays, sequence variants Genomic selection, G×E interaction, polygenic architecture
Organ VIS–NIR/SWIR spectra, biochemical assays Non-destructive trait prediction, stress detection, quality monitoring
Plant / plot RGB and multispectral imagery, 3D point clouds Architectural traits, growth dynamics, phenological stages
Landscape Satellite time series, climate layers, occurrence records Species distribution, habitat connectivity, ecological forecasting

Each scale requires specific methods, but the research program seeks bridges: transfer learning from high-data scales to low-data scales, and fusion architectures that integrate information across levels.

Near-infrared and VIS–NIR spectroscopy

Reflectance spectroscopy provides a rapid, non-destructive window onto leaf and fruit biochemistry. I develop regression models — from partial least squares to convolutional regressors — that predict agronomic traits (water content, sugar concentration, chlorophyll) from spectral profiles, with explicit handling of inter-instrument variability and domain shift between calibration and application conditions. Applications include water stress detection in grapevine and quality monitoring during berry maturation (Ryckewaert et al., 2022; Courand et al., 2022).

Genomic selection and genotype-by-environment modelling

In perennial crops, the long juvenile phase makes early marker-assisted selection particularly valuable. I develop multi-task learning and mixed-model frameworks for genomic estimated breeding value (GEBV) prediction that explicitly model G×E interactions and share statistical strength across related environments. Integrating spectral phenotyping with genomic data via multi-block and multi-modal fusion is an active direction.

Neural networks for species distribution modelling

Classical MaxEnt constrains the predicted distribution to maximum entropy while matching empirical feature expectations — a statistically principled form of regularisation for presence-only data. Embedding this principle in neural architectures allows the model to learn non-linear feature interactions while retaining the theoretical guarantees of MaxEnt against overfitting in data-sparse regions (Ryckewaert et al., 2026). Current extensions address multi-species joint modelling and the incorporation of remote sensing predictors.

Transformer and attention-based architectures

Self-attention mechanisms are naturally suited to spectral and genomic sequences, where local and long-range dependencies coexist. I investigate the application of transformer architectures to 1D spectral regression and to multi-omics integration, with particular attention to attention head interpretability as a route to wavelength-level feature attribution.

Impact

Crop improvement for perennial species. Accelerating breeding cycles through accurate early prediction of performance reduces the time-to-market for new varieties adapted to drought, disease, or changing phenological windows — directly relevant to CIRAD’s mandate for sustainable tropical and Mediterranean agriculture.

Biodiversity monitoring at scale. Calibrated, spatially explicit species distribution maps with uncertainty estimates support habitat management, protected area planning, and reporting under international biodiversity frameworks (CBD, IPBES).

Methodological contributions. Each application problem motivates general methodological questions — about multi-modal fusion, interpretability, and distribution shift — whose solutions are publishable and transferable across biological domains.
